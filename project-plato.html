PLATO

Searching for terrestrial exoplanets and measuring the properties of their host stars with precision space photometry.

Context

PLATO (PLAnetary Transits and Oscillations of stars) is the European Space Agency’s exoplanet-hunting mission. It will monitor the light from more than 200,000 stars, looking for the slight, periodic dimming produced when a planet crosses in front of its star. The same precise measurements will reveal stellar oscillations, allowing astronomers to investigate the structure and age of the stars that host planetary systems.

Scientific objectives

PLATO aims to find and characterise rocky planets, including Earth-sized worlds in orbits extending into the habitable zones of Sun-like stars. Transit light curves provide planetary radii; complementary ground-based radial-velocity observations can provide masses and hence densities. Asteroseismology helps establish the ages and other fundamental properties of host stars. Combining these measurements will clarify how planetary systems form and evolve.

Optical design and payload

PLATO carries 26 wide-field, refractive telescope cameras on a common optical bench: 24 normal cameras arranged in four groups and two fast cameras. Normal cameras collect measurements every 25 seconds, while fast cameras operate at 2.5-second cadence. The array covers more than 2,000 square degrees simultaneously. Achieving precise, stable photometry requires careful optical design, alignment, focus control and verification of structural, thermal and optical behaviour.

Development and status

PLATO’s spacecraft assembly was completed in October 2025. It passed an extensive thermal-vacuum test campaign in ESA’s Large Space Simulator in April 2026, with analysis and further verification continuing before launch. ESA currently plans launch aboard Ariane 6 in April 2027, followed by operations near the Sun–Earth L2 point. PLATO has not yet begun astronomical observations; the scientific capabilities described here are mission goals, not on-orbit results.

Our contribution

ADOPT researchers at INAF Padova, including Demetrio Magrin and Roberto Ragazzoni, contributed to the optical design of the PLATO telescope units. Related work by Davide Greggio and collaborators addresses structural, thermal, optical and performance analysis, while other Padova contributors have worked on alignment, verification and the mission’s scientific preparation. These activities form part of the wider international PLATO Mission Consortium.
