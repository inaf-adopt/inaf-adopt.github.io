NIRVANA-VIS

Visible-light GLAO-assisted speckle holography upgrade for LINC-NIRVANA at the LBT.

Overview

NIRVANA-VIS is a planned visible-wavelength imaging upgrade of LINC-NIRVANA, the adaptive-optics-assisted near-infrared imager at the Large Binocular Telescope (LBT) in Arizona. It aims to extend LINC-NIRVANA’s capabilities into the 600–1000 nm range, combining ground-layer adaptive optics (GLAO) with speckle holography. The upgrade is developed within the Italian PNRR STILES programme and is intended to make use of existing LINC-NIRVANA infrastructure while preserving its near-infrared capabilities.

Adaptive optics and imaging

LINC-NIRVANA uses pyramid wavefront sensors and natural guide stars to correct atmospheric turbulence with the LBT adaptive secondary mirror. Its GLAO mode has demonstrated on-sky point-spread-function improvements of up to a factor of three. NIRVANA-VIS is designed to exploit that correction through short-exposure imaging and speckle holographic reconstruction. The proposed visible channel uses a fast-frame 8K CMOS detector and includes Hα observations. Its design field of view is 52 × 52 arcseconds, sampled at 6.5 milliarcseconds per pixel; near-diffraction-limited reconstructed images are a project objective, not an achieved NIRVANA-VIS performance result.

Scientific objectives

The intended science includes resolving close stellar companions and crowded stellar populations; tracing jets and outflows from young stellar objects through Hα emission; studying dense globular clusters; and investigating stellar environments in nearby galaxies such as M31 and M33. Combining AO-assisted image sharpening with reconstruction should make high-resolution visible imaging possible across a field larger than that of many conventional diffraction-limited modes.

Development and status

The project involves INAF, the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy and the wider LBT collaboration. Work includes opto-mechanical design, detector characterisation, laboratory assembly, integration and verification, optical alignment, non-common-path aberration calibration, control-software integration and data-reduction preparation. The 2026 design work targets possible on-sky operation in 2027, subject to installation approval and successful integration and commissioning. NIRVANA-VIS should therefore be described as under development, not as an instrument already producing routine science results.
